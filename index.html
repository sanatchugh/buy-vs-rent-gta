How this works, what it assumes, and where it can go wrong
The comparison. Both paths begin with the same cash. The buyer puts it into the down payment and closing costs; the renter invests it. Going forward the renter also invests the amount you set under “If you invest instead.” At your horizon the tool compares the buyer's net sale proceeds (home value − mortgage balance − selling costs, tax-free as a principal residence) against the renter's portfolio (contributions grown at your return, minus tax on the gains). The monthly and upfront tables show the cash side so you can see where the money goes.
  • Forced savings count fairly: mortgage principal isn't treated as a cost — it lowers the balance and shows up as equity at sale.
  • Land transfer tax uses Ontario's brackets, plus Toronto's municipal LTT for 416. First-time buyers get up to $4,000 (province) + $4,475 (Toronto).
  • CMHC insurance (4.0% / 3.1% / 2.8% of the loan for <10% / <15% / <20% down) is added to the mortgage; its 8% Ontario PST is paid upfront. 20%+ down needs no insurance.
  • Mortgage renewal at year 6 re-amortizes the remaining balance at your “yrs 6+” rate — this is where rate risk appears.
  • Investment tax defaults to 50% as a proxy for Canada's capital-gains inclusion rate; set it to 0% for a TFSA/FHSA/RRSP. The home's gain is always tax-free.
  • Two ways to invest the difference: Fixed amount invests the same dollar figure every year (simple, but it overstates the renter's savings late on, since rent keeps rising while the mortgage payment doesn't). Actual yearly gap invests the real month-by-month difference on whichever side is cheaper that year — the renter while owning costs more, the buyer once it costs less. The gap mode is the fairer both-sides comparison.
  • Break-even & the grid. “How the answer changes” finds the appreciation rate at which buying exactly ties renting, and maps the verdict across a range of appreciation × investment-return — so you see the whole decision space, not one guess. Compare scenarios lets you pin two full setups (buy now vs wait, condo vs detached, 5% vs 20% down) side by side.
Baked-in fixed assumptions (kept out of the inputs to reduce clutter): a $1,500 one-time moving cost on purchase; a $50/mo condo special-assessment reserve (condo types only); a +0.20% CMHC surcharge on a 30-year insured amortization; one month's rent held as a last-month deposit (returned at the end, earning nothing). The purchase is assumed to be resale, so no HST — a new build adds 13% HST, largely offset by the new-housing rebate (net extra varies, not modelled here). Utilities are excluded on both sides as a cost-of-living item (a house typically costs more to heat and cool than a condo). Property-tax history shown is Toronto; 905 cities differ.

Where this can go wrong. The result is only as good as the two biggest assumptions: home appreciation and investment return. Small changes swing the answer a lot.

Example: a $900k home at the default 3%/yr is worth about $1.04M in 5 years. But GTA prices actually fell ~4.5% in the last year. If instead they drop 2%/yr for 5 years, that same home is worth about $813k — a ~$230k swing that turns a 5-year “buying ahead” into “renting clearly ahead.” Flip it the other way (6%/yr) and buying wins far sooner. Set appreciation to 0% and −2% to see your downside before deciding.
